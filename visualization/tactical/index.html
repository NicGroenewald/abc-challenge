9th ABC Challenge · head-pose forecasting

Where will your head be in 200 ms?

A VR headset has to draw the next frame for where you will be looking, not where you were. We built a model of 7,555 numbers that makes that guess 100 times a second.

A head wearing a VR headset replays a Beat Saber player's recorded head motion at quarter speed. The player is from QuestSet, a public dataset the model never trained on. A translucent ghost headset shows where the model predicts the headset will be 200 ms later. Only the head's rotation is recorded and shown, not its position.

Prediction · what goes in

4 seconds in.

The model is given the last 4 seconds of head orientation: 400 samples, 100 a second. Each one is a quaternion: four numbers (w, x, y, z) that describe a 3D rotation.

Prediction · what comes out

200 milliseconds out.

It must say where the head will point over the next 20 samples. Only the 20th is scored, 200 ms ahead. This is one real input and its answer, drawn to scale, from a player the model never trained on (a Beat Saber session from the public QuestSet dataset, CC BY 4.0). Each of the four numbers is drawn on its own vertical scale.

Entries are judged on accuracy, size, latency and innovation, on people and headsets the model has never seen.

Data · raw

394,171 recordings.

The training data is BOXRR-23, a public collection of people playing VR games. For one batch of our downloads we kept the full log: 394,171 recordings from 9,217 players. Nobody cleaned it: headsets write at different speeds, clocks stick, and in most recordings the head hardly moves.

The dots show proportions: one dot stands for about 394 recordings, and which dots stay lit is not which recordings passed.

The counts in this section are real, from our own download log of BOXRR-23 (Nair et al., CC BY-NC-SA 4.0). No recording from it is shown on this site: its data use agreement does not allow passing them on.

Data · the rate check

Six in ten are the wrong speed.

The model reads exactly 100 samples a second, and the challenge's rule keeps only recordings already between 85 and 110. That leaves 159,099. A few thousand more had no head to follow or nothing left after cleaning.

Data · the motion check

1.9% survive.

A head that hardly moves teaches the model nothing. Only 7,635 recordings swing far and fast enough to pass every check. With at most 5 per player, 2,878 recordings from 1,081 players go on.

Data · the split

Split by player.

The model is judged on strangers, so every player goes into one group only. Together with the players from our other batches, which went through the same checks, the final dataset holds 7,078 players and 204,603,125 training windows.

Data · one model input

One window.

Each training example is one window cut from a recording: 4 seconds in, 400 samples, and the next 200 ms out, 20 samples. The training group holds 204,603,125 of them.

How it got this small

Same idea, one seventh the size.

Built the plain way, this design would store 51,444 numbers. Two changes take it to 7,555.

Results

4.50° off, 200 ms ahead.

Measured on 1,049 players the model never trained on.

The bar to beat. Guess that nothing changes: 6.70° off.

Two guesses need no model at all. Any model has to earn its size by beating them.

These are our own tests on BOXRR-23 players, not the challenge's hidden test. The 20-player benchmark is the smaller test every earlier version was scored on. Its players are easier than average, so every error is lower there. We trained the final model three times and kept the run with the lowest validation error: 4.542°, against 4.553° and 4.546°. That run is the one scored here and shown in the 3D view.

Speed

≈78 µs per prediction.

Timed on a desktop PC, not on the challenge's XR device.

The machine is Nic's PC: an AMD Ryzen 7 5800X3D running Linux, with ONNX Runtime on the processor only, one prediction per call. ≈78 µs is the median of 3,000 timed calls on one thread. Allowed four threads, the median is ≈92 µs. This is not the machine behind the paper's headline timing.

Limits

Fast turns still get away.

On the 1,049 test players, half of all forecasts land within 3.60° of the truth. One in twenty misses by more than 10.90°. The faster the head moves, the larger the miss.

Every recording we trained on is Beat Saber, resampled onto an exact 100-a-second grid. The challenge's test comes from other people and other headsets, at roughly 100 a second with some jitter. And the timing above is a desktop PC, not an XR device.
